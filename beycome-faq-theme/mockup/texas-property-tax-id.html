Texas Property Tax ID Number: How to Find and Use It

Updated April 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Texas has 254 counties — more than any other state — and each one assigns its own property tax ID to every parcel of land within its borders. Whether you're buying a home in Houston, Austin, or a small rural county, understanding this number puts you in control of your property tax records, exemptions, and appeal rights. This guide walks you through exactly how to find your Texas property tax ID, what it means, and how to use it.

What Is a Texas Property Tax ID Number?

In Texas, the property tax ID number is called a property ID, account number, or parcel number, and it is assigned by your county's Central Appraisal District (CAD) — not the county tax assessor-collector. Texas separates the appraisal function from the tax collection function, so two different offices handle your property.

The CAD is responsible for:

  • Assigning the property ID
  • Estimating fair market value each January 1
  • Maintaining the property records database
  • Processing exemptions and appeals

The county tax assessor-collector (or in some cases, a city or school district collector) is responsible for:

  • Calculating the actual tax bill
  • Sending statements
  • Collecting payments
  • Issuing tax certificates

Both offices reference the same property ID, so you can use it to look up information at either office.

How to Find Your Texas Property Tax ID Number

There are several reliable ways to locate your property ID, all free.

1. Check Your Tax Statement

Your county tax assessor-collector mails a tax statement between October and November each year. Your property ID appears near the top, often labeled "Account Number," "Property ID," or "Geo ID." It is the most reliable source for the exact format your county uses.

2. Search Your County CAD Website

Every Texas county has a CAD with a public property search portal. Enter your street address, owner name, or DBA to retrieve your full property record including the property ID. The Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts maintains a directory of all 254 CAD websites.

3. Use the Texas Comptroller's Property Tax Assistance Division

The Texas Comptroller's website offers statewide tools and links for property tax research, including access to state-mandated ratio studies and appraisal data by county.

4. Check Your Deed or Closing Documents

Your deed and closing disclosure contain the legal description and often the property ID or account number assigned by the CAD at the time of closing.

How to Read a Texas Property Tax ID

Texas has no single statewide property ID format — each CAD designs its own. Common formats include:

Abstract-Survey Format

Rural and older Texas properties are often described by abstract number and survey name — a system dating to the original land grants. A property ID might look like A0123-SMITH-J-1.5AC, encoding the abstract, original grantee name, and acreage.

Subdivision-Block-Lot Format

Urban and suburban platted properties use a lot-and-block system. Example: OAKWOOD HEIGHTS BLK 4 LOT 12, encoded into a numeric ID by the CAD.

Sequential Account Number

Large counties like Harris, Dallas, and Tarrant often issue a simple sequential numeric account number (e.g., 0650890000014) that acts as a database key rather than encoding location.

Your CAD's website will show you the exact format used locally.

How Texas Property Taxes Are Calculated

Understanding the math helps you verify your bill and spot errors before the deadline.

Step 1: Appraisal by the CAD

The CAD estimates your property's market value as of January 1 each year. This is a mass appraisal — not an individual appraisal — using comparable sales, cost approach, and income data for commercial property.

Step 2: Appraised Value = Market Value (Usually)

In Texas, appraised value equals market value unless a cap applies. The homestead cap (see below) limits how fast the appraised value can rise for a primary residence.

Step 3: Subtract Exemptions

Applicable exemptions reduce the taxable value. The homestead exemption removes at least $100,000 from the school district portion; other exemptions vary by taxing unit.

Step 4: Apply Each Taxing Unit's Rate

Each taxing unit (school district, county, city, MUD, etc.) sets its own millage rate and calculates its portion of your bill separately. The total tax bill is the sum of all taxing unit bills.

Example: $400,000 appraised − $100,000 homestead exemption = $300,000 taxable × 1.80% = $5,400 school district tax (plus separate county, city, and other unit taxes)

Texas Property Tax Exemptions

Texas offers some of the most generous property tax exemptions in the country. Missing one is money left on the table.

Homestead Exemption

If the property is your primary residence, you qualify for the homestead exemption. As of 2023, the Texas Constitution requires school districts to exempt $100,000 of your home's appraised value. Many counties and cities layer on additional local exemptions of 5–20% of value.

The homestead designation also triggers the 10% appraisal cap — your appraised value cannot increase more than 10% per year, no matter how fast the market rises. This cap can be enormously valuable in a hot real estate market.

Deadline to apply: April 30 of the tax year. File with your county CAD — most accept online applications. You only apply once; it renews automatically.

Over-65 Exemption

Homeowners age 65 or older receive an additional exemption (at least $10,000 from school taxes on top of the homestead exemption) and a tax freeze — school district taxes cannot increase as long as you own and occupy the home. Many counties and cities also offer over-65 exemptions of their own.

Disabled Persons Exemption

Homeowners who qualify as disabled under Social Security or SSI rules receive the same school district exemption as those over 65, including the tax freeze option. You cannot claim both the over-65 and disabled exemption simultaneously.

Disabled Veterans Exemption

Veterans with a disability rating from the VA receive a partial or total exemption depending on the percentage rating:

Agricultural (Ag) and Wildlife Exemptions

Rural Texas landowners may qualify for a special agricultural appraisal based on the land's productivity value rather than market value — often reducing the taxable value by 70–90%. Wildlife management is an alternative qualifying use. This is commonly called an "ag exemption" though technically it is a special valuation method.

How to Appeal Your Texas Property Tax Assessment

Texas property owners have strong appeal rights, and a successful protest can save hundreds or thousands of dollars per year. The process is straightforward but has hard deadlines.

Protest Deadline

You must file your protest by May 15 of the tax year, or within 30 days of the date on your Notice of Appraised Value — whichever is later. Missing the deadline forfeits your right to appeal that year.

How to File

File a Notice of Protest (Form 50-132) with your county CAD. Most CADs now accept online filing through their websites. State the grounds for your protest — most commonly "value is over market value" or "value is unequal compared to similar properties."

Informal Hearing

Most protests begin with an informal meeting with a CAD appraiser. Come prepared with comparable sales data (recent sales of similar homes), a recent independent appraisal if you have one, and photos of any defects or issues. Many protests are resolved at this stage with a reduction.

Appraisal Review Board (ARB)

If you are unsatisfied with the informal outcome, you can present your case to the ARB — an independent panel of citizens. The hearing is formal but relatively simple. The ARB's decision is binding unless you appeal further.

Further Appeals

If the ARB decision is still unsatisfactory, you can appeal to district court, the State Office of Administrative Hearings (SOAH), or binding arbitration. These options are typically used for high-value commercial properties.

Pro tip: Even a modest home protest is worth filing. Many Texas CADs settle 60–80% of protests with a reduction. The filing is free and takes about 10 minutes online.

When Are Texas Property Taxes Due?

Texas property tax bills are mailed between October and November and are due by January 31 of the following year without penalty. A 7% penalty and interest accrues on February 1 if unpaid. Accounts delinquent past July 1 are subject to an additional 20% attorney collection fee.

Payment Options

  • Online through your county tax assessor-collector's website (credit card, e-check)
  • In person at the tax office
  • By mail (postmark by January 31)
  • Installment payments if you qualify (over-65, disabled, or certain other conditions)

Escrow Payments

If you have a mortgage, your lender likely collects 1/12 of your estimated annual tax bill each month into an escrow account and pays the tax directly. Verify your escrow payment is sufficient each year — if your assessed value increases significantly, your lender will adjust the monthly payment.

Bottom Line

Your Texas property tax ID is the gateway to your full appraisal record — value history, exemptions applied, and ownership data — all accessible for free at your county CAD's website. Texas property tax rates are among the highest nationally, but the state's robust exemption system (especially the homestead cap and over-65 freeze) can dramatically reduce what you owe. File your homestead exemption by April 30, watch for your Notice of Appraised Value in April, and protest by May 15 if the value seems high. Those three steps alone can save most Texas homeowners thousands of dollars per year.
