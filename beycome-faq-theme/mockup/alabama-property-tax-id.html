Alabama Property Tax ID Number: How to Find and Use It

Updated April 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Every parcel of real property in Alabama is assigned a unique identification number by the county tax assessor's office. This number — called a parcel number, parcel ID, or property ID — is the key to your property's complete public record: assessed value, tax history, ownership transfers, and exemption status. Alabama has some of the lowest effective property tax rates in the nation, but only homeowners who file the right exemptions capture that advantage. Whether you are buying in Birmingham, reviewing your tax bill in Huntsville, or checking rural land values in Pickens County, this guide shows you exactly how to find and use your Alabama property tax ID.

What Is an Alabama Property Tax ID Number?

An Alabama property tax ID is a county-assigned number that uniquely identifies a parcel of real property within that county's records. It is assigned by the county tax assessor — or in many Alabama counties the Revenue Commissioner, a combined office that handles both assessment and tax collection. The parcel number appears on every official document tied to the property: tax bills, deeds, recorded plats, and closing disclosures.

The parcel number may also be called:

  • Parcel ID or parcel number
  • Property ID or tax account number
  • Map and parcel number
  • GIS parcel number
  • PPIN (Property Parcel Identification Number) in some counties

Alabama's 67 counties each maintain their own numbering format. The parcel number stays with the land across ownership changes and tax years — it is the most stable identifier for researching a property's history.

How to Find Your Alabama Property Tax ID

All four methods below are free.

1. Check Your Property Tax Bill

Alabama counties mail property tax notices in October. Your parcel number is printed on the notice, typically labeled "Parcel ID," "Property ID," or "Map/Parcel." This is the most authoritative source for the exact format your county uses.

2. Search Your County Assessor or Revenue Commissioner's Website

Most Alabama counties offer a free online property search. Enter your street address or owner name to retrieve your parcel ID, assessed value, tax history, and ownership information. Many counties use platforms like qPublic (Schneidercorp), Ingenuity (AssurGIS), or county-built GIS portals.

3. Use the Alabama GIS Portal

The Alabama Department of Revenue and the Alabama GIS office maintain statewide parcel layers that can be queried by address. Some counties participate in the Alabama Maps statewide viewer, which lets you click a parcel on a map to retrieve its ID and assessment data.

4. Check Your Deed or Closing Documents

Your recorded deed references the parcel ID in the legal description. Your closing disclosure, title commitment, and settlement statement also list it — making it easy to locate after purchase.

Alabama's Property Tax Classification System

Alabama uses a property classification system that is fundamental to understanding your tax bill. The state does not tax property at full market value — instead, property is assessed at a fraction of market value called the assessment rate, which varies by property class.

ClassProperty TypeAssessment Rate
Class IUtility property30% of fair market value
Class IIAll other property (commercial, industrial, non-owner-occupied residential)20% of fair market value
Class IIIAgricultural and forest land, historic buildings10% of fair market value
Class IVOwner-occupied residential property10% of fair market value

This means a $300,000 owner-occupied home is taxed on just $30,000 of assessed value (10%), while the same property used as a rental is taxed on $60,000 (20%) — double the tax burden from classification alone. Getting the correct Class IV designation is the single most important step for Alabama homeowners.

Alabama Property Tax Rates by County

Alabama has some of the lowest effective property tax rates in the United States. Tax rates are expressed in mills ($1 per $1,000 of assessed value). Because the assessed value is only 10% of market value for owner-occupied homes, even moderate mill rates translate to very low effective rates.

How Alabama Property Taxes Are Calculated

The calculation follows a clear four-step process.

Step 1: Determine Fair Market Value

The county tax assessor estimates the property's fair market value. Alabama law does not set a fixed reappraisal cycle — counties may reassess on varying schedules, and many rural counties reassess infrequently. If your property has not been reassessed in years, the assessed value may differ significantly from current market conditions.

Step 2: Apply the Assessment Rate

Multiply the fair market value by the appropriate class rate (10% for owner-occupied residential). This produces the assessed value.

Step 3: Subtract Exemptions

Subtract any applicable homestead or other exemptions from the assessed value to arrive at the net taxable value.

Step 4: Multiply by the Millage Rate

Multiply the net taxable value by the total millage rate (state + county + municipal + special districts).

Example: $250,000 market value × 10% = $25,000 assessed value − $4,000 homestead = $21,000 net taxable × 50 mills ÷ 1,000 = $1,050 annual tax

Alabama Property Tax Exemptions

Alabama offers some of the most generous property tax exemptions in the South. Filing the right exemptions can dramatically reduce — or completely eliminate — your tax bill.

Standard Homestead Exemption

Any Alabama homeowner who occupies their property as a primary residence qualifies for the standard homestead exemption. This exempts $4,000 of assessed value from all state, county, and municipal taxes. On a home assessed at $25,000 (10% of $250,000 market value), this eliminates 16% of the taxable base. Apply once at your county tax assessor's office — the exemption renews automatically.

Homestead Exemption for Seniors Age 65 and Older

Homeowners age 65 or older with a net annual income of $12,000 or less (per taxpayer) qualify for an enhanced exemption that eliminates all state property tax on their primary residence. This is one of the most valuable senior exemptions in the nation — qualifying seniors in most Alabama counties pay little to no property tax at all.

Homestead Exemption for Disabled Persons

Homeowners who are totally and permanently disabled qualify for the same enhanced exemption as seniors age 65+ with income under $12,000: complete exemption from state property taxes on the primary residence. A physician's statement of total disability is required.

Homestead Exemption for Blind Persons

Homeowners who are legally blind qualify for an exemption of $4,000 of assessed value from all taxes, regardless of income. This is in addition to any other exemptions for which they may qualify.

Disabled Veteran Exemption

Veterans with a 100% service-connected disability rating are exempt from all state, county, and municipal property taxes on their primary residence. No income limit applies. Surviving spouses of qualifying veterans may continue the exemption. Apply at the county tax assessor's office with VA disability documentation.

Current Use Value for Agricultural and Forest Land

Land actively used for bona fide agricultural or forestry purposes qualifies for Class III assessment at 10% of current use value rather than 10% of market value. Current use value for farmland and timberland is based on productive capacity — typically far lower than market value in growing areas, resulting in substantial tax savings.

How to Appeal Your Alabama Property Tax Assessment

If you believe your property's fair market value is too high, Alabama law gives you the right to appeal. Act within the filing window — it closes each year.

File with the County Tax Assessor

The first step is a written objection filed directly with the county tax assessor or revenue commissioner. You must file between October 1 and December 31 — the same window in which tax notices are issued and taxes are due. Present comparable sales data, a recent independent appraisal, or documentation of property defects that affect value.

County Board of Equalization

If the assessor does not resolve your objection, your appeal goes to the County Board of Equalization. The board reviews the evidence and may adjust the assessed value. Its decision is issued in writing.

Circuit Court

If you disagree with the Board of Equalization's decision, you may appeal to the Circuit Court of your county within 30 days of the board's decision. This step involves formal legal proceedings and is typically used for high-value properties or significant valuation disputes.

When Are Alabama Property Taxes Due?

Alabama property tax notices are mailed in October each year. Taxes are due and payable October 1 through December 31. Bills paid after December 31 are delinquent and subject to a 10% penalty plus interest. Continued non-payment results in a tax lien and eventually a tax sale.

Payment Options

  • Online through your county tax collector or revenue commissioner's website
  • In person at the county courthouse
  • By mail (postmark by December 31)
  • Drop box at the county tax office

Bottom Line

Alabama's property tax system is built around low effective rates, but you only capture those rates if you take two key steps: file for the Class IV designation (owner-occupied residential, 10% assessment rate) and claim your Homestead Exemption as soon as you move in. If you are 65 or older with income under $12,000, your state property taxes may be eliminated entirely. Use your parcel ID to look up your county assessor's records, verify your classification, and confirm your exemptions are applied before the December 31 deadline. And if your assessed value seems high, the October–December window is your only opportunity to file a challenge — don't let it pass.
