California APN Lookup: Prop 13, Assessed Value & Your County Assessor

Updated April 21, 2026 · 8 min read

California property taxes operate under rules that often surprise new buyers: your neighbor in the same house may pay a fraction of what you owe, your county assessor tracks a number called an APN rather than a parcel ID, and your first tax bill after closing can include a "supplemental" charge nobody warned you about. At the center of it all is Proposition 13, passed in 1978, which fundamentally changed how assessed value is calculated — and still does today. This guide explains what an APN is, how to find yours in all 58 counties, how Prop 13 shapes your bill, and what exemptions can reduce it.

What Is a California APN?

Every parcel of real property in California is assigned an Assessor's Parcel Number (APN) — a unique identifier used by the county assessor to track ownership, assessed value, tax history, and exemptions. The APN appears on your property tax bill, deed, title report, and closing disclosure. It is the key to looking up your property's complete public record.

The APN may also be labeled:

  • Assessor's Parcel Number or Assessment Number
  • Parcel number or tax parcel number
  • Assessor's ID or AIN (Assessor's Identification Number — used in Los Angeles County)
  • Folio number (rare, mostly Florida usage but sometimes seen on older CA docs)

How to Read a California APN

California APNs follow a three-part Book–Page–Parcel structure, though the exact digit count and delimiter vary by county:

Book – Page – Parcel
Example (Los Angeles): 2345-016-023
Example (Santa Clara): 158-38-024
Example (San Diego): 123-456-78-00
SegmentWhat It Represents
BookAssessor map book number — covers a geographic area of the county
PageSpecific page within that book — narrows to a neighborhood block
ParcelIndividual lot on that page — your specific property

Los Angeles uses a 10-digit AIN (e.g., 2345-016-023) with no spaces on some systems. San Francisco uses a Block-Lot format (e.g., 3745 – 019). Always use the format shown on your county assessor's portal when searching.

How to Find Your APN in Any California County

Each of California's 58 counties maintains its own independent assessor portal — there is no statewide parcel database. All searches are free.

1. Check Your Property Tax Bill

The county sends annual secured tax bills each October. Your APN is printed prominently at the top, labeled "Assessor's Parcel Number" or "APN." This is the fastest and most authoritative source.

2. Search Your County Assessor's Portal

Enter your street address or APN at the county assessor's website. You'll get your current assessed value (both land and improvements), the base year value locked in under Prop 13, ownership history, and any active exemptions. See the full county list at the end of this article.

3. Check Your Deed or Title Report

Your grant deed and title insurance commitment both reference the APN. After closing, your deed is recorded with the County Recorder (combined with the Assessor in most California counties). Your closing disclosure also lists the APN in the property description section.

4. Use the County GIS Map Viewer

Most California counties offer an interactive parcel map. Click on your property on the map to pull up the APN, assessed value, and a link to the full assessor record. LA County's portal at portal.assessor.lacounty.gov is one of the most comprehensive in the state.

Proposition 13: Why Your Assessed Value Isn't Market Value

This is the most important concept for California property owners to understand — and it explains why two identical homes on the same street can have wildly different tax bills.

When you purchase a property in California, the county assessor sets your base year value equal to the purchase price (or the value at time of change in ownership). Under Proposition 13:

  • The assessed value can increase no more than 2% per year, regardless of what the market does
  • The base rate of property tax is capped at 1% of assessed value (plus voter-approved bonds and assessments)
  • Assessed value is reset to market value only when the property sells or transfers in a taxable change of ownership

The result: a neighbor who bought their home in 1995 for $200,000 may pay taxes on a Prop 13-adjusted assessed value of around $320,000 today, while you — buying the same home in 2025 for $1.4 million — are assessed at $1.4 million from day one. Your tax bill could be 4× higher for identical properties.

This also means that the county assessor's assessed value is not an appraisal. It is a historical, inflation-adjusted number that can diverge significantly from market value over time.

The Supplemental Tax Bill: What New Buyers Miss

When you buy a California home, the county reassesses the property at your purchase price mid-year. The difference between the prior owner's assessed value and your new assessed value is taxed on a prorated basis for the remainder of the fiscal year. The county mails a separate supplemental tax bill within a few months of your purchase.

New buyers are frequently caught off guard by supplemental bills — especially in high-appreciation markets where the previous owner had a very low Prop 13 base. Budget for one or two supplemental bills (sometimes two are issued if the purchase straddles fiscal years) in your first year of ownership.

California Property Tax Rates by County

The base property tax rate is 1% of assessed value statewide. On top of that, counties and local governments levy voter-approved bonds and assessments — school bonds, library bonds, infrastructure assessments — that vary by jurisdiction. The effective rate is typically between 1.0% and 1.3% of assessed value. Counties with heavy school bond debt run higher; rural counties tend to run lower.

New developments often sit within a Mello-Roos Community Facilities District (CFD), which can add 0.3%–1.5% on top of the base rate for 20–40 years to fund infrastructure. Always ask your agent or title company whether a property is in a Mello-Roos district before making an offer.

California Property Tax Exemptions

Homeowners' Exemption ($7,000)

If you occupy your home as your principal residence as of January 1, you qualify for a $7,000 reduction in assessed value — saving roughly $70 per year at the 1% base rate. File a Homeowners' Exemption claim (BOE-266) with your county assessor after purchase. The exemption carries forward automatically once approved; you don't need to reapply each year unless your use changes.

Disabled Veterans' Exemption

Veterans with a 100% service-connected disability (or blind, or a double amputee) qualify for a basic exemption of $100,000 off assessed value (approximately $1,000/year in savings). Low-income disabled veterans may qualify for a $150,000 exemption. Surviving spouses can continue the exemption. File with your county assessor using form BOE-261-G.

Proposition 19 — Senior and Disaster Transfer

Effective February 2021, Proposition 19 allows homeowners who are age 55 or older, severely disabled, or victims of a declared disaster to transfer their Prop 13 base year value to a new principal residence anywhere in California — up to three times in a lifetime. Critically, Prop 19 also tightened the parent-child transfer exclusion: inherited properties now only keep the Prop 13 base value if the heir uses it as their primary residence, and only for the assessed value below $1 million above the current base.

Church, Nonprofit, and Agricultural Exemptions

Property used exclusively for religious, charitable, or scientific purposes may qualify for a welfare exemption. Agricultural land that meets Williamson Act contract requirements is assessed on agricultural use value rather than market value, often resulting in significant tax reduction for rural parcels.

How to Appeal Your California Assessment

If you believe the county assessor has over-assessed your property, you have the right to file an appeal with the county's Assessment Appeals Board (AAB).

When to File

You have two filing windows:

  • July 2 – November 30 — the annual regular filing period (most common)
  • Within 60 days of receiving a supplemental or escape assessment notice

What to Bring

The strongest appeals use recent comparable sales (within 90 days of the lien date, January 1) showing the property was worth less than the assessed value. An independent appraisal is persuasive but not required. Document any physical defects, deferred maintenance, or adverse conditions the assessor may not have observed.

The Prop 13 Floor

Your base year value is a floor — the assessor cannot raise it above the Prop 13-adjusted amount without a change of ownership or new construction. However, if market values drop below your assessed value (as happened in the 2008-2012 downturn), the assessor must temporarily reduce your assessed value to market. If your market value has recovered above your Prop 13 base again, the base takes over. Monitor your assessment notice annually; many counties proactively reduce assessments during market downturns without requiring an appeal.

California Property Tax Payment Schedule

California's fiscal year runs July 1 – June 30. Annual secured property taxes are billed in two installments. The county mails bills in late October each year.

InstallmentDue DateDelinquent AfterPenalty
1st installment (Jul – Dec)November 1December 1010% of unpaid amount
2nd installment (Jan – Jun)February 1April 1010% + $10 cost

The memory trick used by California tax offices: "No Darn Fooling Around" — November, December (delinquent), February, April (delinquent). If both installments are unpaid by June 30, the property becomes tax-defaulted, triggering a 1.5% monthly redemption penalty.

Payment Options

  • Online through your county tax collector's portal (most accept credit/debit and e-check)
  • By mail (postmark by the delinquent date)
  • In person at the county tax collector or finance department
  • Some counties offer automatic bank draft enrollment

Bottom Line

California's property tax system rewards long-term ownership and creates a sharp break at the point of sale. When you buy, your APN is reset to market value under Prop 13 — and that becomes your floor for as long as you own. Your immediate priorities after closing: file for the Homeowners' Exemption to get the $7,000 assessed-value reduction, watch for a supplemental tax bill in the months after purchase, and check whether you're inside a Mello-Roos CFD that adds to your effective rate. If you're 55 or older and planning to move within California, Prop 19 portability can preserve your existing low base year value and save tens of thousands of dollars in taxes on your next home. Use your APN at your county assessor's portal to verify your base year value, confirm any exemptions, and see your complete payment history.
