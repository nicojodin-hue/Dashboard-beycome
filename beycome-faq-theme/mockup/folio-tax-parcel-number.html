What Is a Folio Number, Tax Parcel ID or Parcel Number? The Complete State-by-State Guide

Updated April 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Your property has a unique identifier that follows it forever — through every sale, refinancing, tax bill, and court filing. In Florida it's called a folio number. In California it's an APN (Assessor's Parcel Number). In New York it's an SBL (Section-Block-Lot). In New Jersey it's a Block and Lot. In Texas, South Carolina, North Carolina, Ohio, and most other states it goes by yet another name. But regardless of what your state calls it, every one of these identifiers does the same job: it is your property's permanent tax map address, the number that links your land and building to every public record, assessment roll, deed index, and tax database in your county. This guide explains exactly what it is, what the different names mean, how to find it for free, and what you can do with it — with detailed state-specific guides linked for every state we cover.

Folio Number, Parcel Number, Parcel ID — What's the Difference?

Nothing — they are different names for the same thing. A folio number, parcel number, parcel ID, tax ID number, and property ID all refer to the unique numeric or alphanumeric identifier that a county or municipal assessor assigns to a specific piece of real property. In everyday use, you will encounter all of these terms: a mortgage lender may call it a "parcel number," your county tax bill may say "property ID," your title company may say "folio," and the county GIS map may say "APN." They all point to the same record.

What Is a Folio Number?

A folio number is the term used in Florida for the county property appraiser's parcel identifier. Florida's folio number format varies by county but typically encodes the section, township, range, subdivision, and lot within the county's tax map grid. Miami-Dade, for example, uses a format like 30-4025-001-0010, where each segment identifies a progressively smaller geographic unit down to the individual parcel.

The term "folio" comes from historical land record books — each property was assigned a folio (page) in the county's ledger. The name stuck even as tax records went digital. Outside Florida, you will almost never hear the word "folio" in a property tax context — but if you are buying or selling in Florida, it is the number you need for everything: searching the county property appraiser's website, filing a homestead exemption, appealing your assessment, or pulling a title search.

What Is an APN (Assessor's Parcel Number)?

An APN is California's term for the parcel identifier. California APNs are assigned by the county assessor and typically follow a three-part format: Book-Page-Parcel (e.g., 123-456-78). The book and page originally referred to the assessor's map books; the parcel number identified the specific lot on that page. California's APN is the anchor for Proposition 13 base year values — every property's assessed value is tied to its APN from the year of acquisition, and the APN follows the property through every subsequent sale.

What a Parcel ID Is Actually Used For

Your parcel ID is required in almost every official transaction involving your property:

  • Tax bills: Your county mails property tax bills keyed to the parcel ID. Without it, payments may be misapplied.
  • Deeds and titles: Every recorded deed references the parcel ID alongside the legal description. Title insurers use it to pull the full title chain.
  • Mortgage applications: Lenders require the parcel ID to order an appraisal, pull flood zone data, and confirm property tax liabilities.
  • Assessment appeals: You cannot file an appeal with a county Board of Taxation, Board of Review, or Tax Court without citing the exact parcel ID.
  • Permits and zoning: Building departments link permits to parcel IDs, not just addresses. A search of your county permit history requires the parcel ID.
  • HOA and utility records: Many homeowners associations and utility districts index their records by parcel ID.
  • FEMA flood maps: The FEMA flood zone determination for a specific parcel requires the parcel ID or legal description.

How to Find Your Parcel Number for Free

You do not need to pay a third party for your parcel number. It is public information available from at least three free sources:

1. Your Property Tax Bill

The fastest source. Every county mails property tax bills with the parcel ID prominently displayed. If you pay through escrow, ask your mortgage servicer for a copy of the most recent tax bill — it includes the parcel number, assessed value, and tax rate.

2. Your County Assessor's or Appraiser's Website

Every county in the United States maintains a public property search portal. Search by street address and you will retrieve the parcel ID, owner name, assessed value, tax history, and often a scanned property record card. The URL format is usually countysomething.gov/assessor or similar. The state-specific guides below link directly to every county portal for 15 states.

3. Your Deed

The deed recorded when you purchased your home contains the legal description of the property and typically the parcel ID (or the information needed to derive it from the tax map). Deeds are recorded with the county recorder, register of deeds, or — in Connecticut — the town clerk. Most are available free online through the county's document search portal.

4. Title Commitment or Closing Disclosure

If you have purchased or refinanced in the past several years, your closing package includes a title commitment that lists the parcel ID prominently. Check your closing binder or ask your title company.

How Property Assessment Works

Once your parcel ID is assigned, the assessor uses it to track your property's assessed value — the value used to calculate your tax bill. The relationship between assessed value and market value varies dramatically by state:

The ratio matters because it determines your tax bill. A $500,000 home assessed at 35% (Ohio) produces a taxable value of $175,000. The same home assessed at 100% (Florida) produces $500,000. The tax rate is set to match the ratio, so effective bills are comparable — but when the ratio drifts from the statutory level, assessments become non-uniform and appeal opportunities open up.

Universal Property Tax Exemptions to Know

Most states offer some version of these four exemptions. The application is keyed to your parcel ID:

Homestead Exemption

A reduction in assessed value or tax liability for owner-occupied primary residences. Almost every state offers one. Florida's homestead exemption reduces assessed value by up to $50,000. Texas's is $100,000 off school district taxes. California's is $7,000 off assessed value for the Homeowners' Exemption. Filing requirements and deadlines vary by state — see the state guides below.

Senior / Elderly Exemption or Freeze

Most states reduce property taxes for homeowners over 65 who meet income thresholds. Programs range from percentage reductions (New York's § 467 up to 50% off) to full freezes (New Jersey's Senior Freeze, which reimburses any increase above a base-year amount). These require annual or periodic renewal with your local assessor.

Veterans' Exemption

Available in every state, but the benefit level varies enormously — from a $250/year deduction in New Jersey to a 100% exemption for totally disabled veterans in Virginia, New York, and others. Typically requires a one-time filing with a copy of DD-214.

Agricultural / Use-Value Exemption

Farmland, timberland, and open space in most states can qualify for assessment at use value rather than market value — dramatically reducing the tax burden on rural acreage. Common programs: Florida's Agricultural Classification, California's Williamson Act, Pennsylvania's Clean and Green (Act 319), Connecticut's § 12-107, and Tennessee's Greenbelt Law. A rollback tax applies if the land is converted to another use.

How to Appeal a Property Tax Assessment

If your assessed value is higher than market value, you can appeal. The process varies by state but follows the same general pattern:

  1. Gather comparable sales — 3–5 similar properties that sold in the past 6–12 months, priced below what your assessment implies. Pull these from Zillow, the MLS, or your county's public sales database.
  2. Check the assessor's property record card — errors in square footage, bedroom count, or condition are common and easy to correct without a formal hearing.
  3. File with the local review board — every state has a first-level appeal body (Board of Equalization, Board of Assessment Review, Board of Taxation). Filing fees are minimal ($5–$50). Deadlines range from April 1 (New Jersey) to June (California).
  4. Escalate if needed — state tax courts, administrative tribunals, or small-claims equivalents (New York's SCAR, for example) handle appeals the local board denies.

A successful appeal can reduce your annual tax bill for the current year and — in some states — trigger refunds for prior years. On a $500,000 home in a high-tax state, a 10% assessment reduction is worth $1,000–$2,500 per year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my parcel number the same as my lot number?

Usually no. Your lot number refers to the subdivision plat recorded with the county — it appears in your deed's legal description as "Lot 14, Block 3, Sunrise Estates." Your parcel number (or folio, APN, SBL, etc.) is the assessor's internal database key, assigned independently and formatted differently. The two numbers coexist in your county's records but serve different purposes. You need the parcel number for tax-related matters; you need the lot number (legal description) for deed-related matters.

Does my parcel number change when I sell my home?

The parcel number stays with the land, not the owner. When you sell, the buyer receives the same parcel number. It only changes if the parcel is subdivided, merged with an adjacent parcel, or re-platted. In states like California, however, the assessed value tied to that APN resets to purchase price — the number itself doesn't change, but the assessment history restarts.

Can I look up another property's parcel number?

Yes — property tax records are public in every state. You can search any address in your county's online assessor portal and retrieve the parcel ID, owner name (as of the last assessment), assessed value, and tax history. There is no fee and no login required for most county portals.

My parcel number has letters — is that normal?

Yes. Condominiums, co-ops, and subdivided lots often have alphanumeric qualifiers. In New Jersey, condo units use a qualifier like C0001. In California, fractional APNs like 123-456-78-1 indicate a sub-parcel. In Pennsylvania, some counties use letter suffixes. The format is unique to each county's map system.

What if my parcel number is wrong on my tax bill?

Contact your county assessor immediately. A wrong parcel number on a tax bill means the payment may be credited to the wrong account — or to a non-existent one. Bring your deed and the tax bill to the assessor's office and request a correction before the payment due date. Keep the receipt.

Bottom Line

Whether your county calls it a folio number, APN, SBL, block-and-lot, or parcel ID, it is the single most important number attached to your property. It is your entry point to the county assessor's database, the key to filing exemptions and appeals, and the identifier used by lenders, title companies, and courts. Find it on your tax bill, look it up free on your county's website, and use it every time you need to verify, challenge, or act on your property's tax record.
