Georgia Property Tax ID Number: How to Find and Use It

Updated April 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Every parcel of land in Georgia has a unique property tax ID number — sometimes called a parcel ID, parcel number, or tax parcel number. This number is the key that unlocks your property's public tax records, assessment history, and ownership information. Whether you're buying a home, disputing an assessment, or just checking what your neighbor paid, this guide shows you exactly how to find and use it.

What Is a Georgia Property Tax ID Number?

A Georgia property tax ID number — officially called a parcel identification number (PIN) or tax parcel number — is a unique code assigned by your county tax assessor's office to identify a specific piece of real property. Every county in Georgia maintains its own numbering system, so the format varies from one county to the next, but the purpose is always the same: to distinguish your parcel from every other parcel in that county.

The number appears on your:

  • Annual property tax bill
  • Property deed
  • County tax assessor's notice of assessment
  • Title insurance commitment
  • HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure at closing

Unlike a street address, which can change, or an owner name, which changes with every sale, the parcel ID stays with the land permanently (unless the county rezones or subdivides it).

How to Find Your Georgia Property Tax ID Number

There are four reliable ways to locate your parcel ID in Georgia.

1. Look at Your Tax Bill

The fastest method. Your county mails an annual property tax bill that prominently displays your parcel number near the top of the statement. It may be labeled "Parcel ID," "PIN," "Tax Map / Parcel," or simply "Account Number" depending on your county.

2. Search the County Tax Assessor's Website

Every Georgia county operates a public property search portal. Enter your street address and the system returns your parcel ID along with assessed value, tax history, and owner information — all at no cost. Georgia counties use three main platforms:

  • qPublic.net — used by more than 100 Georgia counties (search by address, owner name, or parcel ID)
  • Schneider GIS / qPublic Schneider — used by Dawson, Hall, Jones, Lumpkin, Rockdale, and others
  • County-specific portals — Fulton, DeKalb, Cobb, Gwinnett, and other large counties run their own systems

3. Use the Georgia Superior Court Clerks' Cooperative Authority (GSCCCA)

The GSCCCA maintains a statewide deed index at gsccca.org. Search by grantor/grantee name or county to find your recorded deed, which contains the parcel ID and legal description. This is especially useful if you've recently purchased and haven't received a tax bill yet.

4. Check Your Closing Documents

If you purchased the property, your title commitment, deed, and closing disclosure all contain the parcel ID. Look for a section titled "Legal Description" or "Property Identification."

How to Read a Georgia Parcel ID Number

Georgia does not use a single statewide parcel ID format — each county defines its own structure. That said, most follow one of two common patterns.

Map-Block-Parcel Format

Many counties encode geographic location directly into the ID. For example, a parcel ID like 14-112-04-002 might break down as:

SegmentMeaningExample
14Tax district or land lot districtDistrict 14
112Land lot numberLand Lot 112
04Block or subdivision sectionBlock 4
002Individual parcelParcel 2

Sequential Account Number Format

Some counties — particularly urban ones — assign a simple sequential account number with no embedded geography. DeKalb County, for instance, uses a 15-digit number that acts as a database key rather than a geographic code.

When in doubt, your county tax assessor's website will explain the format it uses.

Georgia Property Tax Rates by County

Property taxes in Georgia are calculated by multiplying the assessed value (40% of fair market value) by the millage rate set each year by the county, school board, and any special taxing districts. One mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value.

Formula: Fair Market Value × 40% × (Total Millage Rate ÷ 1,000) = Annual Property Tax

Georgia Homestead Exemption

If the property you own is your primary residence, you almost certainly qualify for the Georgia homestead exemption — a direct reduction in the assessed value used to calculate your tax bill.

Standard Statewide Exemption

The base Georgia homestead exemption removes $2,000 from the 40%-assessed value before calculating county and school taxes. On a $300,000 home, that saves roughly $50–$90 per year depending on your millage rate — modest on its own, but most counties layer on additional local exemptions.

Local Exemptions

Many counties offer substantially larger exemptions. For example:

  • Fulton County — additional $30,000 exemption on county taxes for qualified homeowners
  • DeKalb County — $12,500 exemption from school taxes for qualifying residents age 62+
  • Cobb County — $10,000 exemption from school taxes; additional senior exemptions available
  • Forsyth County — up to $15,000 exemption for residents over 65

Senior and Disability Exemptions

Georgia law provides several additional exemptions for homeowners 62 and older, 65 and older, disabled veterans, and surviving spouses. Some counties offer full school tax exemptions for qualifying seniors — meaning a senior homeowner could eliminate the largest portion of their tax bill entirely. Check with your county tax assessor to see what you qualify for.

How to Apply

You must apply for the homestead exemption by April 1 of the tax year for it to take effect that year. File with your county board of tax assessors — most counties accept online applications. You only need to apply once; the exemption renews automatically as long as the property remains your primary residence.

How to Look Up Any Property's Tax Records in Georgia

With a parcel ID — or even just a street address — you can access the full public record for any Georgia property. Here is what you can find for free:

  • Ownership history — current and prior owners, sale dates, sale prices
  • Assessment history — how the assessed value has changed over the years
  • Tax payment history — whether taxes are current or delinquent
  • Property characteristics — square footage, lot size, year built, number of bedrooms/bathrooms
  • Exemptions applied — which exemptions reduce the taxable value
  • Maps and aerial photos — boundary maps, satellite imagery

This information is public record and freely available. You do not need to pay a third-party site to access it.

How Georgia Property Taxes Are Calculated

Understanding the calculation helps you verify your bill and catch errors.

Step 1: Determine Fair Market Value

The county tax assessor estimates the fair market value of your property as of January 1 each year. This is an appraisal — not necessarily what you paid, but what the assessor believes the property would sell for on the open market.

Step 2: Calculate Assessed Value

Georgia law requires assessors to value property at 40% of fair market value. A home the assessor estimates at $350,000 fair market value has an assessed value of $140,000.

Step 3: Subtract Exemptions

Any applicable homestead or other exemptions are subtracted from the assessed value to produce the net taxable value.

Step 4: Apply the Millage Rate

Multiply the net taxable value by the total combined millage rate (county + school + city + any special districts), then divide by 1,000.

Example: $140,000 assessed − $2,000 exemption = $138,000 × (30 mills ÷ 1,000) = $4,140 annual tax

How to Appeal Your Georgia Property Tax Assessment

If you believe your property has been overvalued, you have the right to appeal. Georgia's appeal process has strict deadlines — act quickly if you receive a notice of assessment you disagree with.

45-Day Deadline

You have 45 days from the date of the assessment notice to file an appeal. This deadline is firm — missing it means you cannot appeal that year's assessment.

Three Appeal Options

  • County Board of Equalization (BOE) — free informal hearing before a local board; the most common first step
  • Arbitration — binding arbitration using an independent appraiser; results are final
  • Superior Court — full judicial appeal; typically reserved for high-value properties or complex disputes

What to Bring

Build your case with recent comparable sales data (homes like yours that sold for less than what the assessor implies), a recent independent appraisal, and documentation of any physical defects or conditions that reduce value. Your county's qPublic portal is a good starting point for pulling comparable assessments.

How to Pay Georgia Property Taxes

Georgia property taxes are administered at the county level, and due dates vary. Most counties send bills between August and October with a due date of December 20. Some counties operate on a different cycle — always check with your county tax commissioner.

Payment Methods

  • Online via your county tax commissioner's website (credit card or e-check)
  • In person at the county tax commissioner's office
  • By mail (check payable to the county tax commissioner)
  • Drop box at the county courthouse

Delinquent Taxes and Tax Sales

If property taxes go unpaid, the county can place a fi.fa. (tax lien) on the property. After 12 months, the county may sell the tax lien — and eventually the property itself — at a public tax sale. Delinquent tax records are public and searchable using the parcel ID on most county portals.

Bottom Line

Your Georgia property tax ID number is one of the most useful pieces of information you can have as a homeowner or buyer. It unlocks your property's full public record — assessment, ownership, taxes paid, and more — in seconds, at no cost. Find yours on your tax bill, your deed, or by searching your county assessor's portal. If you think your assessment is too high, you have 45 days from the notice date to appeal. And if you haven't claimed your homestead exemption yet, file before April 1 — it's one of the simplest ways to reduce your annual tax bill.
