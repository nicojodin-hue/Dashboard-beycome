Ohio Property Tax ID Number: How to Find and Use It

Updated April 21, 2026 · 7 min read

Every parcel of real property in Ohio is assigned a unique parcel number by the county auditor's office. This number — sometimes called a parcel ID, permanent parcel number (PPN), or tax parcel number — is the key to your property's complete public record: appraised value, taxable value, tax history, ownership transfers, and exemption status. Whether you are buying in Columbus, reviewing your tax bill in Cleveland, or checking farmland values in rural Holmes County, this guide shows you exactly how to find and use your Ohio property tax ID.

What Is an Ohio Property Tax ID Number?

An Ohio property tax ID is a county-assigned number that uniquely identifies a parcel of real property within that county's records. It is assigned by the county auditor — the official responsible for property valuation and tax administration in Ohio. The parcel number appears on every official document tied to the property: tax bills, deeds, deed transfers, recorded plats, and closing disclosures.

The parcel number may also be called:

  • Permanent Parcel Number (PPN)
  • Parcel ID or parcel number
  • Tax account number
  • Tax map number
  • Auditor's parcel number

Unlike a street address, the parcel number is tied to the land itself and does not change when the property is sold, the street is renamed, or ownership structures change. It is the most reliable identifier for tracking a property across tax years and ownership transfers.

How to Find Your Ohio Property Tax ID

All four methods below are free.

1. Check Your Property Tax Bill

Ohio counties mail property tax bills twice a year (winter and summer). Your parcel number is printed on the bill, usually labeled "Parcel Number," "PPN," or "Parcel ID." The winter bill covers the first half of the year and the summer bill covers the second half — both reference the same parcel number.

2. Search Your County Auditor's Website

Every Ohio county auditor operates a free online property search portal. Enter your street address or owner name to retrieve your parcel number, appraised value, taxable value, tax history, and ownership information. Most county auditor sites are maintained through platforms like Auditor's website (county-specific), the statewide Ohio Real Estate Information System (OREIS), or third-party tools like Beacon by Schneider Geospatial.

3. Search the Ohio Department of Taxation Resources

The Ohio Department of Taxation oversees property tax policy statewide and provides links to each county auditor. While it does not maintain a unified parcel lookup, it publishes exemption forms, appeal instructions, and county contact information.

4. Check Your Deed or Closing Documents

Your recorded deed and closing disclosure reference the parcel number in the legal description. The title commitment and HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure also list it, making it easy to locate after purchase.

Cuyahoga County (Cleveland) uses a format like 001-12-345, while Hamilton County (Cincinnati) uses a longer numeric string. Always use the format shown on your county auditor's portal — parcel numbers are not interchangeable between counties.

Ohio's Unique Property Tax Assessment System

Ohio uses a distinctive two-step assessment system that sets it apart from most other states.

True Value vs. Assessed Value

The county auditor estimates each property's true value — an estimate of market value. Ohio law then requires that property be assessed at exactly 35% of true value. This assessed value (called the "taxable value" before credits) is what the millage rate is applied to.

Example: $300,000 true value × 35% = $105,000 assessed value

Ohio counties must conduct a full sexennial reappraisal (every 6 years) and a triennial update at the midpoint. The reappraisal involves mass appraisal of all properties; the triennial update adjusts values based on sales data without a full on-site review.

Tax Reduction Credits

Ohio law mandates two automatic credits that reduce every property owner's tax bill before the final amount is calculated:

CreditWho QualifiesReduction
10% RollbackAll residential and agricultural property10% off total tax
2.5% Rollback (Owner Occupancy Credit)Owner-occupied residential propertyAdditional 2.5% off

These rollbacks are paid by the state of Ohio directly to the county, so the homeowner sees a lower bill without doing anything. Owner-occupied homeowners effectively receive a combined 12.5% automatic reduction on their annual tax bill.

Ohio Property Tax Rates by County

Ohio property tax rates are expressed in mills — one mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value, or equivalently $1 per $1,000 of true value at 35% assessment. Because of the 35% assessment ratio, effective rates relative to market value are lower than the mill rate suggests.

How Ohio Property Taxes Are Calculated

The calculation involves four steps. Understanding each helps you verify your bill and identify errors before the payment deadline.

Step 1: Determine True Value

The county auditor estimates the property's true (market) value through a mass appraisal process during each sexennial reappraisal or triennial update. You can find your property's true value on your county auditor's website.

Step 2: Calculate Assessed Value (35%)

Multiply the true value by 35% to get the assessed value. This is the base on which taxes are calculated.

Step 3: Subtract Exemptions

If you qualify for the Homestead Exemption or other exemptions, the exempt amount is subtracted from the assessed value before the tax rate is applied.

Step 4: Apply the Millage Rate and Rollbacks

Multiply the net assessed value by the total millage rate, then subtract the 10% rollback and (if owner-occupied) the 2.5% rollback.

Example: $300,000 true value × 35% = $105,000 assessed value × 60 mills ÷ 1,000 = $6,300 gross tax − 12.5% rollbacks = ~$5,513 net annual tax

Ohio Property Tax Exemptions

Ohio offers several programs that can significantly reduce or defer your property tax bill.

Homestead Exemption

Ohio's Homestead Exemption is one of the most valuable relief programs for qualifying homeowners. If you are:

  • Age 65 or older, OR
  • Totally and permanently disabled, OR
  • The surviving spouse of a qualifying homeowner (age 59 or older at time of spouse's death)

…and the property is your primary residence, you qualify to have the first $26,200 of true value (adjusted annually for inflation) excluded from taxation. This reduces your assessed value by $9,170 (35% of $26,200), saving most qualifying homeowners $200–$500 per year depending on the county.

There is no income limit for homeowners who turned 65 before January 1, 2014. For those who turned 65 on or after that date, an income limit applies (currently $36,100 per year for the standard exemption). Apply at your county auditor's office by December 31 of the year you first qualify. The exemption renews automatically.

Enhanced Homestead Exemption for Disabled Veterans

Veterans with a 100% service-connected disability rating — or those receiving 100% disability compensation due to individual unemployability — qualify for an enhanced Homestead Exemption that excludes the first $52,300 of true value (double the standard amount). No income limit applies. Surviving spouses of qualifying veterans may also be eligible.

Owner Occupancy Credit (2.5% Rollback)

If you own and occupy your home as your primary residence, you automatically receive a 2.5% credit on top of the standard 10% rollback. This credit is applied by the county auditor — you do not need to apply separately as long as the property is classified as your primary residence.

Current Agricultural Use Value (CAUV)

Ohio farmland actively used for agriculture can qualify for taxation based on its Current Agricultural Use Value (CAUV) rather than market value. CAUV is calculated based on soil type, productivity, and capitalized crop income — values that are typically far below market value in areas where farmland is being developed. This program provides substantial tax savings for qualifying agricultural landowners. A rollback tax applies if the land is converted to non-agricultural use within a set period.

Other Exemptions

  • Nonprofit and religious organizations — Properties used exclusively for charitable, educational, or religious purposes may be fully exempt
  • Government property — Federal, state, and local government property is exempt
  • Historic preservation — Some counties offer tax incentives for designated historic structures

How to Appeal Your Ohio Property Tax Assessment

If you believe your property's true value is too high, Ohio law gives you the right to appeal. The process moves through several levels.

Deadline

You must file a complaint with the county Board of Revision (BOR) by March 31 of the year following the tax year in question. For example, to appeal your 2025 tax year value, file by March 31, 2026. Missing this deadline forfeits your right to appeal for that year.

County Board of Revision (BOR)

The BOR consists of the county auditor, county treasurer, and a member of the county commissioners. Submit a written complaint (DTE Form 1) with your parcel number, your estimate of true value, and supporting evidence — comparable sales, a recent independent appraisal, or documentation of property defects. The BOR schedules a hearing and issues a written decision. Many appeals are resolved at this level.

Board of Tax Appeals (BTA)

If you disagree with the BOR's decision, you can appeal to the Ohio Board of Tax Appeals, a state agency. Appeals must be filed within 30 days of the BOR's decision. The BTA hears evidence and issues written orders. The BTA's decision can be further appealed to the Ohio Court of Appeals or the Ohio Supreme Court.

When Are Ohio Property Taxes Due?

Ohio property taxes are paid in two installments per year:

InstallmentDue DateCovers
First halfFebruary 14 (or last business day before)Prior year first half
Second halfJuly 31 (or last business day before)Prior year second half

Exact due dates vary slightly by county — always check your county treasurer's website for the current year's deadlines. Late payments accrue a 10% penalty on the unpaid amount, and continued delinquency can result in a tax lien and ultimately a tax certificate sale.

Payment Options

  • Online through your county treasurer's website (credit/debit card or e-check)
  • In person at the county treasurer's office
  • By mail (postmark by the due date)
  • Drop box at the county courthouse
  • Some counties offer monthly prepayment plans through the treasurer

Bottom Line

Ohio's property tax system has two things working in your favor: the automatic 10% rollback (12.5% if you're an owner-occupant) cuts every residential tax bill before you do anything, and the county auditor's online portal makes it easy to verify your true value, assessed value, and tax history with your parcel number in hand. The most important action for qualifying homeowners is to file for the Homestead Exemption before December 31 — it applies to every future tax year automatically once approved. If your property was recently reappraised at a value that seems too high, file a complaint with the County Board of Revision by March 31 — comparable sales data is usually enough to secure a reduction without a formal hearing.
