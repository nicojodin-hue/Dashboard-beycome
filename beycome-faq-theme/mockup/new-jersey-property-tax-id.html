New Jersey Property Tax: Block-Lot System, ANCHOR Rebate & How to Appeal Your Assessment

Updated April 21, 2026 · 8 min read

New Jersey consistently ranks as the state with the highest effective property tax rate in the nation — averaging over 2% of market value annually, with some suburban counties running well above that. Behind those numbers is a complex system: 564 municipalities each set their own tax rate, 21 counties each run their own Board of Taxation, and a state-managed equalization process attempts to level the playing field between towns that assess at wildly different percentages of true value. Understanding how your block-lot number connects to your assessment, how equalization ratios affect your appeal rights, and how to claim the ANCHOR rebate and Senior Freeze can easily save New Jersey homeowners thousands of dollars per year.

What Is a New Jersey Property Tax ID?

New Jersey properties are identified by a Block and Lot number (sometimes with a Qualifier) assigned by the municipal tax assessor. This is your property's permanent identifier in the municipal tax records, appearing on your tax bill, deed, title commitment, and the official tax map filed with the municipality.

The full property identifier includes:

  • County (by name or FIPS code)
  • Municipality (by 4-digit code in state databases)
  • Block — a grouping of parcels on the municipal tax map
  • Lot — the individual parcel within that block
  • Qualifier (optional) — used for condominiums, co-ops, and partial lots (e.g., C0001 for condo unit 1)

Block and lot numbers are assigned by the municipal tax assessor and are not the same as lot numbers in a recorded subdivision plat (which appear on your deed description). Always use the tax map block-lot when communicating with the assessor, tax collector, or county board of taxation.

How to Find Your New Jersey Block-Lot Number

1. Check Your Property Tax Bill

New Jersey municipalities mail quarterly tax bills (due February 1, May 1, August 1, November 1). Your block, lot, and qualifier appear on every bill alongside the assessed value, tax rate, and due amounts. This is the fastest way to confirm your property ID.

2. Search the NJ MOD-IV Database

The New Jersey Division of Taxation publishes property assessment data through the MOD-IV database, accessible via the NJ Tax List Search. Search by address to retrieve the block, lot, owner, assessed value, and sale history for any property in the state.

3. Search Your Municipal Tax Map

Every New Jersey municipality maintains a tax map that is a legal document. Many municipalities post their tax maps and assessment search tools on their municipal website. Search for "[your town] tax assessor property search" to find the local portal.

4. Check Your Deed

New Jersey deeds reference the block and lot number. Deeds are recorded with the County Clerk (not a municipal office). The deed legal description will include the block and lot, which is the same identifier used by the tax assessor.

How New Jersey Property Assessment Works

Assessment at True Value

New Jersey law requires all real property to be assessed at 100% of true value (fair market value) — the same standard Connecticut uses. In practice, most municipalities have not conducted a comprehensive revaluation in years or decades, so assessments frequently drift below true value. The state accounts for this through the equalization ratio.

The County Equalization Ratio

Each year, the New Jersey Division of Taxation calculates an average ratio of assessed value to true value for every municipality. This ratio — published in the Table of Equalized Valuations — tells you what percentage of market value a town's assessments represent. A town with a 75% ratio is assessing at 75 cents on the dollar; a town with a 50% ratio is at half value.

True Value Implied by Assessment = Assessed Value ÷ Equalization Ratio
Example: Assessment $300,000 ÷ ratio 0.75 = Implied true value $400,000

Chapter 123 and the 15% Range Rule

When you appeal your assessment, New Jersey's Chapter 123 (N.J.S.A. 54:3-22) governs what the county board must do with the equalization ratio. The board applies this test:

  • Calculate the common level range: the municipality's equalization ratio ± 15%
  • If your implied true value (assessment ÷ ratio) falls within that range, the board will dismiss your appeal even if your assessment is above market value
  • Only if your implied true value falls above the upper limit of the range does the board have authority to reduce your assessment

This "15% cushion" is why many appeals fail on the merits: even an assessment that appears too high may survive review if the ratio-adjusted value falls within range. A strong appeal requires documented comparable sales evidence, not just an opinion that the value is wrong.

New Jersey Property Tax Relief Programs

ANCHOR — Affordable New Jersey Communities for Homeowners and Renters

The ANCHOR program replaced the legacy Homestead Benefit and is New Jersey's largest property tax relief initiative. Eligible homeowners receive a direct payment (not a credit on the bill) based on income:

  • Income up to $150,000: $1,500 benefit
  • Income $150,001–$250,000: $1,000 benefit
  • Renters with income up to $150,000: $450 benefit

You must have owned and occupied your home as a primary residence on October 1 of the benefit year. Apply online at the NJ Division of Taxation — ANCHOR. Applications typically open in the fall for the prior year's taxes.

Senior Freeze — Property Tax Reimbursement (PTR)

The Senior Freeze (PTR) reimburses eligible senior and disabled homeowners for the increase in property taxes over their "base year" amount. Once enrolled, your effective tax liability is frozen at the base year level — the state reimburses any increase above that baseline:

  • Must be age 65+ (or receiving Social Security disability) by December 31 of the application year
  • Must have lived in New Jersey for the past 10 years continuously
  • Income limit: approximately $150,000 (adjusted periodically)
  • Must have paid all property taxes by June 1 of the application year

File Form PTR-1 (first-time filers) or PTR-2 (continuing filers) with the Division of Taxation by October 31.

Veterans' Property Tax Deduction

New Jersey veterans who served honorably in active wartime service receive a $250 annual deduction from their property tax bill. This is applied directly at the municipal level — file a one-time application (Form V.S.S.) with your municipal tax assessor. 100% disabled veterans may qualify for a full property tax exemption. Surviving spouses of qualifying veterans may retain the deduction.

Disabled Persons Deduction

Property owners with a permanent and total disability may qualify for an annual $250 deduction from their property tax bill, similar to the veterans' deduction. File the application with the municipal tax assessor.

How to Appeal Your New Jersey Assessment

Step 1: Review Your Assessment Notice

Every year by February 1, New Jersey municipalities must notify property owners of any change in assessment. If you receive a notice of increased assessment — or if you believe your current assessment is above market value — the appeal clock starts here.

Step 2: File with the County Board of Taxation

File your appeal with the County Board of Taxation by April 1 of the tax year (or within 45 days of the mailing of the Chapter 75 notice, whichever is later). The filing fee is nominal (typically $5–$25). Your appeal must include:

  • The completed appeal form (available from the county board)
  • A statement of your estimated true value
  • Supporting evidence (comparable sales from within 12 months, an appraisal, or documented property condition issues)

The board schedules a hearing and issues a decision. If successful, your assessment — and your tax bill — is reduced for the current year.

Step 3: Tax Court of New Jersey

If the county board denies your appeal (or if your assessed value exceeds $1,000,000, in which case you must file directly at Tax Court), you may appeal to the Tax Court of New Jersey within 45 days of the county board's judgment. Tax Court is a formal legal proceeding; most high-value appeals involve attorneys and certified appraisers. The court's decisions can result in significant multi-year refunds on over-assessed properties.

New Jersey Property Tax Payment Schedule

New Jersey property taxes are billed and due quarterly:

QuarterDue DateGrace Period
1st quarterFebruary 110 days (pay by Feb 10)
2nd quarterMay 110 days (pay by May 10)
3rd quarterAugust 110 days (pay by Aug 10)
4th quarterNovember 110 days (pay by Nov 10)

Interest on late payments accrues at 8% per annum on the first $1,500 of delinquency and 18% per annum on any amount above $1,500. Municipalities add a 6% penalty on delinquencies over $10,000. If taxes remain unpaid for two years, the municipality may sell the tax lien at a tax sale — a process that can ultimately result in loss of the property. Most mortgage servicers escrow NJ property taxes precisely because of these stakes.

Added and Omitted Assessments

If you complete a new construction, addition, or improvement to your property mid-year, the assessor may issue an added assessment — a supplemental bill for the value of the improvement for the portion of the year it existed. Similarly, if property value was inadvertently omitted from the roll, an omitted assessment may be issued for up to three prior years. Both can be appealed to the county board within 45 days of the notice date.

Bottom Line

New Jersey's property taxes are the highest in the nation, and the system's complexity — 564 municipal assessors, 21 county boards, annual equalization ratios, and a Chapter 123 appeal filter — means most homeowners are paying more than they need to. The keys: confirm your block-lot number and assessed value annually, benchmark it against the municipal equalization ratio, and file with the county board by April 1 if the math points to over-assessment. Apply for ANCHOR every fall (even if you think you don't qualify — income limits are generous), and if you're a senior, enroll in the Senior Freeze before your taxes climb further. A $1,500 ANCHOR payment plus a successful assessment appeal can easily cut your effective bill by $3,000–$5,000 per year.
